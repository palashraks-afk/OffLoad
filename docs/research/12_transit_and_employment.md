# 12. Does transit access change who works? (read 2026-10-25)

How to read this note: "Read" means I opened the page. "Via search summary" means I only saw a search-engine summary of the page, so treat it as a lead, not a verified fact. Limits are stated for each item.

## Findings

**1. A randomized trial of transit subsidies in Washington, DC (Phillips, 2014).**
- Source: David C. Phillips, "Getting to work: Experimental evidence on job search and transportation costs," *Labour Economics* 29 (2014) 72-82. Read the author's summary on the World Bank Development Impact blog: https://blogs.worldbank.org/impactevaluations/taking-the-bus-to-opportunity-guest-post-by-david-phillips
- Design: randomized experiment. 468 clients of a nonprofit employment agency (Jubilee Jobs) in far Southeast DC; all got standard job-search help; the treatment group also got two SmarTrip cards with a combined $50 balance.
- Result: probability of finding work within 40 days rose 9 percentage points (26% to 35%). At the 90-day follow-up the gap narrowed to 5 points and was not statistically significant. The blog describes search-intensity gains as mostly insignificant; the journal abstract (via search summary) reports about 19% more applications and interviews in the first two weeks.
- Limits (the author's own): small sample, short follow-up, low statistical power, one treatment package, an actively job-seeking group in a city with a reasonably good transit system. It cannot say anything about long-run effects.
- What it supports: a small, short-lived push to job search from cheaper transit. It does not show that transit access creates lasting jobs.

**2. A natural experiment: Hurricane Sandy and neighborhood unemployment (Tyndall).**
- Source: Justin Tyndall, "Waiting for the R train: Public transportation and employment," *Urban Studies* 54(2), 2017, 520-537. Via search summary of the abstract only (full text not read).
- Design: Sandy cut subway access to some New York neighborhoods and not others; the study compares unemployment.
- Result (abstract): a significant causal effect linking transit access to neighborhood unemployment, especially for groups that depend on transit. Effect sizes not read.
- Limits: one city, one disruption, abstract only.

**3. Car access looks more important than transit access (Blumenberg and Pierce).**
- Source: ACCESS Magazine, "A Driving Factor in Moving to Opportunity" (2016) and a related Welfare-to-Work voucher study (2017). Via search summary; the ACCESS page text did not load.
- Summary: keeping or gaining a car was associated with a higher chance of being employed; better transit access was associated with keeping a job but not with gaining one; the authors say they cannot tell whether the car came before or after the job.
- Limits: correlation, not experiment; low-income adults in specific programs.
- **Why this matters for the app:** do not claim that better bus service creates jobs. The honest claim is narrower: a worker who cannot get home after a late shift cannot take or keep that shift.

**4. Transit reach and job access in large US metros (Brookings, 2011).**
- Source: Tomer, Kneebone, Puentes, Berube, "Missed Opportunity: Transit and Jobs in Metropolitan America," 12 May 2011. Read: https://www.brookings.edu/research/missed-opportunity-transit-and-jobs-in-metropolitan-america
- Read figures: the typical metropolitan resident can reach about 30 percent of jobs in their metro area by transit within 90 minutes; nearly 70 percent of large-metro residents live in neighborhoods with some transit service.
- A summary on New Geography (https://www.newgeography.com/content/002251-transit-the-4-percent-solution, via search summary) says Riverside-San Bernardino's 45-minute job access was 1.3 percent, the lowest of 29 metros over 2 million people (average 5.6 percent, Boston 12.6). I did not find the 45-minute figure on the Brookings page itself.
- Limits: 2011 data, whole metro, all hours of day (not night), "some service" is not "service at the hour you need."
- **Do not use** the earlier "81% of low-income Riverside-area residents ... under 7% of low- and middle-skill jobs" line (from a congressional survey report): the Brookings page I read does not contain it.

**5. Late-shift workers rely on transit and have fewer options (Urban Institute, citing APTA).**
- Source: https://www.urban.org/features/unequal-commute (read). It states that over 790,000 late-shift workers, disproportionately low-income people of color, rely on public transportation to get to work but have far fewer options, citing an American Public Transportation Association report ("Supporting Late-Shift Workers: Their Transportation Needs and the Economy"). A related APTA figure on the page: average commute times for late-shift workers who take transit are twice those of workers with car access. The page gives no year for the APTA report; the Urban feature was published in 2020. The APTA report page itself returned HTTP 403, so the number is verified only as the Urban Institute's statement.
- The regional analysis covers Baltimore, Lansing, Nashville and Seattle (not San Bernardino). In an earlier read of the same page (this project, 2026-10-24) the third-shift test used trips arriving 10:30-11:30 p.m. against peak arrival 7:30-8:30 a.m.
- Limits: four metro areas; national figure undated.

**6. Employment and transit service cuts or strikes.** Searches did not find a rigorous US study of the employment effect of bus service cuts or strikes. Not established; do not claim it.

## What the app can honestly say
- Late-shift workers depend on transit and face worse access at night (items 5, and the Urban Institute's four-city analysis).
- Cheaper transit nudged job search and short-run job finding in one randomized trial, with caveats (item 1).
- It cannot say that service changes cause job gains or losses in San Bernardino County.
