# Deep evidence on the problem (2026-10-25)

Each line says what was read, what it shows, and its limit. Numbers were read from the source page on this date unless marked "via".

## 1. Who works when buses do not run
- **15% of US workers usually work a non-daytime schedule** (5% evenings, 3% nights; the rest rotating, irregular or other). Bureau of Labor Statistics, Job Flexibilities and Work Schedules, 2024-25 averages. Limit: national, not local.
- **Over 790,000 late-shift workers rely on public transportation** and are disproportionately low-income people of color. Urban Institute, "The Unequal Commute", citing an APTA report on late-shift workers. Limit: I read it through the Urban Institute page; the APTA report itself did not load, and the year is not stated on the page.
- **Transit access at night is worse than at rush hour.** Urban Institute compared jobs reachable in 30 minutes by transit arriving 7:30-8:30 am against third-shift arrival 10:30-11:30 pm in several cities (Lansing, Baltimore, Nashville and others). Limit: San Bernardino was not one of the cities I saw.

## 2. The Inland Empire
- County commute averages **32.8 minutes** (California 29.7, US 27.2); **74.5% drive alone** (2024). San Bernardino County Community Indicators, from ACS.
- Brookings (via Rep. Takano's 2018-era Inland Empire survey report): **81% of low-income residents of the Riverside metro live in the suburbs and can reach under 7% of low- and middle-skill jobs by public transit.** Limit: old, "Riverside metro" includes San Bernardino, and I read it secondhand in a congressional office report (survey of 548 people in CD-41, not CA-33).
- Same survey: 32% said their commute took over an hour; over 60% rated it unsatisfactory or terrible. Limit: self-selected respondents in Riverside, not a random sample.
- UC Riverside (Inland Empire Labor and Community Center) report on logistics work exists and describes workers commuting long distances; I found no transit-specific figure in it.

## 3. What a bad commute costs a body
- **PNAS 2016 (Lee et al.)**: 16 night-shift workers drove on a closed track after a night of work and after a night of sleep. **11 near-crashes occurred in 6 of 16 post-night-shift drives (37.5%); none after sleep**; 7 of 16 post-night drives were stopped early for safety. The paper cites 9.5 million Americans working overnight or rotating shifts. Limit: 16 people, closed track, daytime drives; it shows risk exists, not how many real crashes it causes.
- CDC MMWR 2012 on short sleep by shift (already in the app).

## 4. What a bad commute costs an employer
- Unscheduled absence is often quoted at about $3,600 per hourly employee per year (Kronos/Circadian-style figure repeated by trade sites). Limit: not specific to transportation, and the original study was not located. **Do not use in the app.**
- Transportation as a barrier to work: in a 2022 South Carolina survey, almost 20% of able-to-work people who were not working cited transportation (via Richmond Fed). Limit: one state; not about shifts.

## 5. Walking to and from the stop
- Pedestrian deaths near bus stops are higher at midblock stops on high-speed arterials in a 2025 study (reported as roughly 4.7 times higher; I could not open the article, so the exact figure and its comparison group are unverified). Do not quote the number until the paper is read.
- A Delhi case-control study found well-lit bus stops were protective at night. Limit: a different country and road system.
- Local: the app's own count is 217 pedestrian/cyclist deaths inside CA-33, 2019-2023 (FARS).

## 6. What the evidence does NOT establish
- No source here measures how many people in CA-33 lose a job because of the bus schedule.
- No source shows that a trip-planning tool changes outcomes.
- The 790,000 and Brookings figures are old or secondhand. Use "about" language and cite them as given.

## What this means for the entry
The strongest honest story: a large group works non-daytime shifts, buses run least when they need them, the ride and the walk carry measurable health risk, and nobody gives that worker (or their boss) one place to check it. The weakest part is local proof of job loss; say so, and ask users and employers to help measure it.
