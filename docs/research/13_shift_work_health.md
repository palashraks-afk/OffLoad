# 13. Night- and shift-work health (read 2026-10-25)

"Read" = opened the page. "Via search summary" = only a search-engine summary was seen.

**1. Short sleep by shift (CDC, 2012).** Read: https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6116a2.htm (MMWR, 27 April 2012, "Short Sleep Duration Among Workers - United States, 2010").
- Data: 2010 National Health Interview Survey; 15,214 employed adults; response rate 60.8%. Short sleep = an average of 6 hours or less in 24 hours.
- Prevalence (95% CI): all workers 30.0% (29.2-30.9); regular day shift 28.8% (27.8-29.9); regular night shift 44.0% (38.8-49.4); "other shift" (evening, rotating, other combined) 31.6% (29.7-33.6).
- Night shift by industry: transportation and warehousing 69.7% (50.8-83.7); health care and social assistance 52.3% (42.9-61.6).
- Limits (the report's): sleep self-reported in whole hours; broad industry groups; differences may be confounded by age and race/ethnicity; low response rate. The transportation-warehousing interval is wide (50.8-83.7), so quote it as "about 70%" with the range.

**2. Driving after a night shift (PNAS, 2016).** Lee et al., "High risk of near-crash driving events following night-shift work," PNAS (2016). Read earlier in this project from the paper page (https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1510383112).
- Design: 16 night-shift workers drove on a closed track twice, after night work and after a night of sleep.
- Result: 11 near-crashes in 6 of 16 post-night-shift drives (37.5%); none after sleep; 7 of 16 post-night drives were stopped early for safety. All near-crashes came after at least 45 minutes of driving. The paper cites more than 9.5 million Americans working overnight or rotating shifts.
- Limits: 16 people, closed track, daytime drive. It shows risk exists, not how many real crashes it causes.

**3. Cancer classification (IARC, 2019/2020).** Read: https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-monographs-volume-124-night-shift-work
- Night shift work: Group 2A, "probably carcinogenic to humans" (announced 2 June 2020 for Volume 124; the Lancet Oncology summary was July 2019, via search summary).
- Basis: limited human evidence (breast, prostate, colon and rectum cancers), sufficient evidence in experimental animals, strong mechanistic evidence.
- Limits: a hazard classification, not a risk size. It says nothing about how much risk a given worker carries.

**4. Heart disease (Vyas et al., BMJ 2012).** Via search summary (the PubMed page did not load): https://www.bmj.com/content/345/bmj.e4800
- Pooled 34 observational studies, 2,011,935 people. Shift work vs not: myocardial infarction risk ratio 1.23 (95% CI 1.15-1.31); coronary events 1.24 (1.10-1.39, high heterogeneity); ischaemic stroke 1.05 (1.01-1.09); no increase in mortality.
- Limits: observational, varied definitions; cannot show causation. **Verify against the BMJ page before quoting.**

**5. Long commute after work: not established.** I did not find a study measuring crash risk for the commute home after a night shift by bus compared with driving. The PNAS result supports "driving after a night shift is risky"; it does not directly measure what taking the bus changes. The NIOSH page I opened (nurse work-hours training, module 3) is about errors on long shifts, not commutes.

## What the app can honestly say
- "Night-shift workers are more likely to sleep 6 hours or less: 44.0% vs 28.8% for day workers (CDC, 2010 data)."
- "In a small track study, 6 of 16 night-shift drivers had near-crashes on the drive home; none did after a normal night's sleep (PNAS, 2016)."
- It should not say that a bus ride is safer than driving, or quote cancer or heart risks for the individual.
