# How OffloadWatch can lead to real change (and what it cannot claim)

## The honest claim
OffloadWatch makes a hard-to-read public record visible, comparable and easy to act on. It does **not** by itself reduce ambulance delays or save lives, and the submission should not say it does. Delays fall when hospitals, EMS agencies and funders change how patients move through ERs. This app's job is to put the right number in front of the people who can do that, and to make asking for change easy.

## The mechanism (who does what, because of the app)
| Who | What the app gives them | Action it makes easier |
|---|---|---|
| EMS agencies (e.g. Inland Counties EMS Agency) | Their hospitals ranked by ambulance-hours lost (a minimum), the trend, and the agency table for all of California | Raise it at the monthly agency/hospital meeting; show the trend to hospital executives |
| Hospitals | Their own handoff times and share on target, with a clear source | Check the numbers; EMSA already tells listed hospitals to carry out an offload-reduction plan, so this shows whether it is working |
| Members of Congress and county supervisors (and staff) | A one-page district report card, statewide totals, "ambulance-equivalents" | Ask EMSA/agencies for a plan; cite the numbers in letters and hearings |
| Residents | Plain-language numbers for hospitals near them and a pre-written, editable message | Ask their representative or agency a specific question |
| Analysts and journalists | Clean monthly data in CSV, a lower-bound method, a scenario tool (set a time limit and your own cost per ambulance-hour) | Do their own analysis without extracting PDFs |

Why the framing matters: "ambulance-hours lost" and "ambulances out of service all month" convert a percentile in a PDF into capacity, which is what budgets and staffing discussions run on. All of it is a **minimum**; the true figure is higher.

## What is true today (all from the data in this repo)
- Statewide, February 2026: at least 3,636 ambulance-hours lost, about 5.4 ambulances out of service all month, 160 hospitals over their local target.
- CA-33: at least 38.2 hours in February 2026, up from 24.3 in December. Community Hospital of San Bernardino: 90th percentile 47 minutes against a 25-minute target, 60.2% of handoffs on target.
- The newest EMSA report found is February 2026 data. The app labels itself out of date and checks monthly for new reports.

## What would count as evidence of impact (and how to get it)
Do not claim an effect you have not measured. Real, checkable evidence, in order of strength:
1. **Use by a decision-maker**: someone at an EMS agency, hospital, county or Member's office opens it, cites it, or asks for changes because of it. Get this in writing (an email reply is enough) and quote it.
2. **Data corrections**: a hospital or agency uses the "report a data error" link. This shows real scrutiny of the numbers.
3. **Actions taken**: a hospital or agency shares an offload-reduction plan after being asked.
4. **Outcomes**: whether handoff times in your district fall in later months. A drop would be a *correlation*; this app cannot show it caused the drop. The pipeline will show the trend automatically as new months arrive.

## What not to say
- "Reduces delays", "saves lives", "improves response times".
- Anything that ranks hospitals as good or bad. Handoff time reflects crowding, not quality of care.
- Anything implying the app should help someone choose a hospital in an emergency. The 911 crew chooses.

## Prior work to credit
Some counties already publish their own offload dashboards (for example San Diego, Orange, Alameda and Sacramento). EMSA publishes statewide monthly PDFs. OffloadWatch's contribution is a statewide, by-agency and by-congressional-district view in plain language, with a method that states its limits, plus ways to act on it.
